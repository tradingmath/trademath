How the stop price is worked out

For a buy, the stop sits below your entry. For a sell, it sits above.

Buy stop = Entry − (Entry × stop %) ÷ 100
Sell stop = Entry + (Entry × stop %) ÷ 100

On a ₹500 entry with a 1% stop, the distance is ₹5. A buy stop goes at ₹495. A sell stop goes at ₹505. The quantity then comes from your risk: ₹1,00,000 capital at 1% risk is ₹1,000, and ₹1,000 ÷ ₹5 is 200 shares. The target at 1:2 is ₹10 away, so ₹510 for a buy.

Choosing the stop distance

The calculator does not pick the stop for you, and no formula can. A few honest rules of thumb:

  • Fixed percentage stops are simple, but blind. A 1% stop is generous on a quiet large-cap and far too tight on a volatile mid-cap. The same percentage means very different things on different shares.
  • Chart-based stops are usually better. Put the stop just beyond a level that, if broken, tells you the idea was wrong: below a recent swing low for a buy, for example. Then let the position size adjust to fit, as the calculator does.
  • Volatility-based stops adapt. Many traders use a multiple of the average true range, such as twice the ATR. Our stop-loss placement guide goes through the three methods with examples.
  • Never move the stop further away after you are in. The risk you calculated is gone the moment you do.

What the stop does not guarantee

A stop-loss order turns into a market order once the stop price trades, so your fill can be worse, especially at the open, after news, or in a thin share. If the price gaps past your stop, you lose more than you planned. A stop-loss limit order avoids a bad fill but may not execute at all. Whichever you use, keep the risk small enough that one gap does not hurt badly.

Charges are also not in the numbers above. On a tight stop they can be a noticeable part of the loss. The TradeMath calculator can show an estimate when you turn fees on in Settings.

Common questions

How do I calculate a stop-loss price?

For a buy, subtract your stop distance from the entry. For a sell, add it. The distance can be a percentage of the price or a fixed rupee amount per share.

What percentage stop is normal?

Intraday traders often use between 0.5% and 2%, and swing traders use wider stops. It depends on the share's volatility more than on any rule, so look at how far the price normally moves in a day.

How many shares should I buy for a given stop?

Divide the rupees you are willing to lose by the loss per share at your stop. The calculator above does that for you.
