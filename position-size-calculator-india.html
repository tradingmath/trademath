How to calculate position size (with examples for Indian traders)

Position size is how many shares or lots you buy so a stop-loss costs only what you planned. Here is the formula, worked examples for stocks and options, and the mistakes to avoid.

The one formula you need

Position size answers one question: how many shares or lots can I buy so that, if my stop-loss is hit, I lose only the amount I decided to risk?

Quantity = (Capital × Risk %) ÷ (Entry price − Stop-loss price)

The top of the formula is your risk in rupees. The bottom is how much you lose per share if the stop is hit. Divide the first by the second and you get the quantity that keeps the loss inside your limit.

Worked example: a stock trade

  • Capital: ₹1,00,000
  • Risk per trade: 1% = ₹1,000
  • Buy at ₹500, stop-loss at ₹495, so you lose ₹5 per share if stopped out

Quantity = ₹1,000 ÷ ₹5 = 200 shares. The trade value is 200 × ₹500 = ₹1,00,000. If the stop-loss is hit you lose about ₹1,000, which is 1% of your capital, plus brokerage and charges. If your target is twice the risk (₹510), the gross profit is about ₹2,000.

Notice what drives the answer: the distance to your stop, not how confident you feel. A wider stop means fewer shares. A tighter stop means more shares. The risk in rupees stays the same.

What risk % should you use?

There is no single right answer, but most risk-focused traders keep it small, often between 0.5% and 2% of capital per trade. The reason is simple arithmetic: after a string of losses, a small risk per trade leaves you with capital to continue. Losing 1% ten times in a row costs about 9.6% of your account. Losing 10% ten times costs about 65%, and the gain needed to get back is far larger than the loss.

Pick a number you can accept losing without it changing your next decision, and keep it fixed.

Position size for futures and options (lots)

In F&O you cannot buy part of a lot, so the formula uses the lot size:

Lots = Risk in ₹ ÷ (Entry − Stop-loss) ÷ Lot size

Round the answer down. Example: you buy a Nifty option at ₹100 premium with a stop at ₹80, a ₹20 loss per unit. With a lot size of 65, one lot risks 20 × 65 = ₹1,300. If your risk budget is ₹1,000, even one lot is above your limit. You can skip the trade, widen your budget on purpose, or choose a cheaper option. Do not quietly buy one lot and call it 1% risk.

Lot sizes are set by the exchange and are revised from time to time. See the list of NSE F&O lot sizes.

Leverage and margin are not risk

Leverage (for example 5× intraday) changes how much margin you must keep, not how much you can lose to your stop. Your risk is set by the quantity and the stop distance. Leverage only decides whether you have enough margin to hold that quantity. If the margin needed for your calculated quantity is higher than your capital, reduce the quantity or skip the trade. See also: risk-reward ratio explained.

Do charges matter?

Yes, especially for short trades with tight stops. Brokerage, STT, exchange charges, SEBI fees, stamp duty and GST reduce your profit and add to your loss. On a ₹5 stop, a few rupees of charges per share is a noticeable slice of the risk. A calculator that includes charges shows the real net result.

Common mistakes

  • Sizing by capital, not by stop. Buying "₹50,000 worth" tells you nothing about what you lose at your stop.
  • Moving the stop further away after entry to avoid being stopped out. The loss then exceeds the plan.
  • Ignoring gaps. A stop is not a guarantee. If a stock opens far below your stop, the loss can be larger. Keep risk small enough to survive that.
  • Risking more after a loss to win it back. Keep the same percentage.

Common questions

How do I calculate position size in the Indian stock market?

Divide the rupees you are willing to lose by the loss per share at your stop-loss. For example, risking ₹1,000 with a ₹5 stop gives 200 shares. Capital × risk % gives the rupees at risk.

What is a good risk percentage per trade?

Many traders risk between 0.5% and 2% of their capital per trade so that a losing streak does not damage the account. The right figure is the one you can stick to consistently.

Does leverage change my position size?

Leverage changes the margin you must hold, not the risk of the trade. Size the trade from your stop-loss first, then check that you have the margin for it.

How do I size an options trade?

Work out the loss per unit between your entry premium and your stop premium, multiply by the lot size, and divide your risk amount by that. Round the number of lots down.
